My Connection to Bear Studios

I've been part of Bear Studios since my freshman fall, where I've worked on 4 full-stack mobile app and website projects. I've been in the role of both a tech consultant and a project manager, and through these project experiences, I've learned a lot about the connection between design and technical implementation, as well as the differences between doing work for class vs. doing real work for clients.

Interview Portion

What does good design mean to you?

Lydia Park · Design

Good design is when the interface makes sense to the user quickly before they have to stop and think. The visuals should be appealing and guide the user's attention, and the way the user interacts with the website should feel expected and intuitive. If a button looks clickable, it should be.

You shouldn't have to explain how to use the website or app to the user when they look at it for the first time; they should be able to figure it out on their own. I also think good design respects the user. If someone has to second-guess themselves just to finish a simple task, the design is not doing its job.

Lily Sun · Tech

A good design should be intuitive and easy to use, and it should be tailored to the audience. You always have to keep the end goal in mind, whether you're making something that is meant to inform a general audience or persuade specific people. On the technical side of things, good design should be something that can be built and maintained easily, and I like it when the branding is consistent and there are reusable components. There's a balance between being creative and making something that looks visually appealing vs. making something that is usable and works well.

What is your experience in Bear Studios? How is working in the classroom different from working with clients on real projects?

Lydia Park · Design

At Bear Studios I have worked on ChiChi, and I am currently on Silent Surgical to help with web design and SPARC for Skandy. The biggest difference compared to class is that the work has real constraints and stakeholders, so any decisions you make carry more weight.

In the classroom, the assignments are built for learning, and you can take bigger creative risks because the audience is mostly your professor and other students in the class. With clients, you have to understand their goals, preferences, and timeline. They often have a clear vision in mind for what they want, and you have to work within those constraints, even though my job as a consultant is still to make suggestions and advocate for good design. However, it works best when you first listen to what the client wants and then present a few options that fit their comfort level and their needs.

Lily Sun · Tech

On the tech side, the stakes feel higher because people rely on what you ship. In class you can say it works on your laptop, but in client work you need something stable, readable, and easy to maintain for whoever touches it next. You have to be really careful about edge cases and do a lot of testing to make sure everything works as expected.

Also, the client will sometimes have a specific platform in mind for the project. For example, on Panome Bio we had a website project where the client wanted everything to be in WordPress. The client requested some pretty complex scrolling interactions as well, but the WordPress platform is built around templates and plugins, and they had existing setup that limited what we could do cleanly. Projects like that teach you how to explain trade-offs withoutgetting lost in technical details, and how to deliver something the client can actually keep using and updating. It's also a good way to practice communicating with the client and understanding their needs and constraints without getting lost in technical details.

How do you balance visual appeal with usability and technical feasibility?

Lydia Park · Design

As a designer, I think that visual appeal and usability are interconnected, but I'd say I always think about usability first and visual appeal second. If something looks great but users don't understand how to use it, I treat it as a design problem rather than a user problem and consider how I can rework the structure of the interface to be more intuitive, and then build the branding and aesthetics around that. I always try to keep technical feasibility in mind as well, and I'll go over my designs with developers to make sure they can actually build it.

Lily Sun · Tech

I try to separate the goal from the exact effect. If the goal is that a page should feel dynamic, we can usually get there without heavy animation or media that slows everything down.

I have seen designs with lots of unique shadows, custom scroll behavior, and background video. In one case we replaced the video with a static hero and cut the scroll animation. To keep the page from feeling flat, we used a lighter fade-in on scroll. It was faster to build, loaded faster, and still matched the intended vibe.

What's your favorite Figma feature?

Lydia Park · Design

Auto layout because it forces me to design in a way that behaves like real UI. When text changes or when you resize for mobile, the layout still holds together and everything will grow or shrink automatically. You can also nest auto layouts inside of each other, which creates a logical hierarchy that mimcs how developers will actually build the interface. It's basically like flexbox but for designers.

Lily Sun · Tech

I really like the prototyping feature in Figma. It helps me get a feel for the app and understand how you navigate between screens and how the user interacts with the app. You can make components interactive so that when you hover over something like a button, it changes color or size. You can also make overlays show up, and you get a lot of control over the transitions because designers are able to specify the duration and adjust easing curves. Inspecting these properties removes the guesswork when I have to implement a UI.

It also makes client conversations easier because they can click through something real and give clearer feedback than they can from static mockups.

How do you explain your work to a client without jargon?

Lydia Park · Design

We meet with the client and do demos. I show a few options and explain the differences in plain language, like what people notice first, what action is easiest, and what that means for the client’s goals.

If I need to explain spacing or hierarchy, I connect it to outcomes like faster scanning, fewer misclicks, or an overall calmer feel. That tends to land better than using super techincal design concepts, and it helps the client feel confident in our work and decisions.

Lily Sun · Tech

I'd say developers use the same approach as what Lydia just talked about. We meet with the client and do demos. I walk through the most current version of the app or website (what's pushed to GitHub) and usually split-screen to have my code ready. I'll keep my demo focused on the visuals and other things the client cares about, like speed, cost, or privacy concerns. If they're looking for a more technical explanation, I can point out which blocks of code translate to which components on the frontend or what queries are being made, or what script is needed to make a certain interaction work.

Final takeaways

Lydia Park · Design

Client work made me more intentional, and I think I have more empathy now as a result of my work with Bear Studios. I still care about aesthetics, but I think more about clarity, like what the user should notice, what they should do next, and what could confuse them.

If I can't explain a design decision simply to a client, I take that as a sign that I need to refine it. It usually means the idea is not clear enough yet.

Lily Sun · Tech

I would say to stay adaptable, and prioritize simple and reliable solutions. I would rather ship something consistently works than build something too complex that the client can't support after the app/website launches and the project ends. By simplifying my solutions, that makes the product easier to maintain and less risky over time, and it builds client trust in our work.
