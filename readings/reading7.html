Reading Response 7: Do Artifacts Have Ethics?

Monday, April 13th

Reading Link

In the reading, Sacasas pushes back against the idea that tech companies can simply add a moral layer to their products. He instead argues that ethics are already built into every tool we use. Similar to Sacasas, I disagree with Om Malik's observation that "we in tech don't understand the emotional aspect of our work," especially as a CS student who has done a lot of frontend development and work on user-facing products. What struck me was his critique of the mindset that "guns don't kill people." This mindset is comforting because it lets us believe that technology stays neutral until a person misuses it. Sacasas reframes the question entirely, urging us to think about how a certain device reshapes us over time. He had a long list of questions like "What habits will this instill?" and "What practices will it displace?" When we apply these questions to a gun, we see that a gun allows the user to kill or hurt someone quickly at a distance with little physical strength, unlike a hammer. Carrying a gun might make a person feel more powerful or paranoid and change how they interpret body language. It also changes power dynamics, and owning a gun might displace the need to de-escalate or call for help. When I try to apply Sacasas's list of questions to my own life, it made me realize how often I ignore the slow, quiet influence of technology like my phone or laptop, or when I use social media. I used to think that ethics in technology was about protecting user privacy and preventing any harm, but the deeper issue is actually formation. Every artifact is subtly teaching me how to see the world and spend my time, and the real question isn't whether or not artifacts have ethics, it's more about whether I notice.
